**2/** **@AgileJebrim** ^2097673978487648648

Tbh this mindset is actually one of the few useful things I learned in university. I have an ABET-accredited Software Engineering degree and spent multiple semesters learning actual engineering principles rather than just Computer Science.

The professor for Requirements Engineering in particular drilled into us hard about identifying the user’s problem (which they often don’t know) and solving it, always maintaining bidirectional traceability with every commit (every change must have a specific requirement it fulfills, all kept within the NFR constraints). People have a nasty habit of getting distracted and wasting time on things that don’t actually address the user’s problem.

The Software Architecture course focused specifically on how to translate the NFR constraints into quality attributes that governed the design of the architecture. You don’t even establish a path forward unless you’ve first established technical feasibility. I spent years intermittently doing research first to ensure I had a reasonable technically feasible path forward on achieving isochronous execution in a data parallel environment before I began development on a tool project to guarantee delivering it.

Yes, a proper engineering discipline will help. And also properly planning your execution even before you write a single line of code.

"Here is the 2 week sprint with User Stories and some UML. Let's play this theater"

Now the entire software industry is trying to undo the damage (some are unfortunately still smitten with these ideas)
